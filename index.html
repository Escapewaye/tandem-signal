01 / BUSINESS · FIELD NOTES

Before you build a business, test the problem.

A strong business idea starts with a real problem—not a logo, a website or a complicated plan.

Read the article

It is tempting to begin with the visible parts of a business: a name, colors, social accounts and a polished website. Those things can help later. First, find out whether anyone actually needs what you want to offer.

1. Describe the problem in one sentence

Be specific about who has the problem, what makes it frustrating, and what they currently do about it. “People need help with marketing” is broad. “Independent tutors struggle to turn one-time students into repeat bookings” is testable.

2. Look for existing behavior

Search forums, reviews, community discussions and competitor feedback. Pay attention to people spending time or money to solve the problem already. Complaints are clues, but repeated behavior is stronger evidence.

3. Talk to potential customers

Ask about their last experience with the problem. What did they try? What did it cost? What was still missing? Avoid leading questions like “Would you buy my idea?” Compliments are not the same as demand.

4. Offer the smallest useful solution

Before building a large product, test a simple service, sample, checklist or paid pilot. Set a clear price and see whether someone is willing to take the next step.

The signal: Validate the problem and willingness to pay before investing heavily in the solution.
02 / TECHNOLOGY · PRACTICAL AI

Use AI like a capable assistant—not an autopilot.

AI can speed up research and production, but a useful workflow still needs human judgment.

Read the article

AI tools are good at turning a clear brief into a first draft, organizing information, generating alternatives and handling repetitive steps. They can also confidently produce errors, outdated claims or details that sound plausible but are unsupported.

1. Give it a defined job

Instead of asking “build my business,” ask for a competitor comparison, a customer interview script, a first draft of an FAQ or a checklist for reviewing a landing page. Narrow tasks are easier to evaluate.

2. Supply context and constraints

Explain the audience, goal, tone, budget, deadline and what the tool must not assume. Ask it to label unknowns rather than fill gaps with guesses.

3. Verify important claims

Check numbers, laws, prices, product features, quotations and health or financial claims against reliable primary sources. Do not publish an AI answer simply because it reads smoothly.

4. Keep a human approval step

Review the output for accuracy, originality, privacy and usefulness. Never paste private customer data or confidential information into a tool unless you understand its data handling and have permission.

A simple repeatable workflow

  • Brief: define one outcome.
  • Draft: ask AI for a structured first version.
  • Check: verify facts and remove unsupported claims.
  • Improve: edit for clarity and usefulness.
  • Approve: publish only after a person reviews it.
The signal: Automate repetition, not responsibility. AI should increase your capacity—not replace your judgment.
03 / MONEY · MONEY BASICS

Revenue is not profit—and profit is not cash.

Knowing the difference can prevent a growing business from running out of money.

Read the article

Three numbers can tell very different stories. Revenue is the money earned from sales before expenses. Profit is what remains after the relevant expenses are subtracted. Cash flow tracks money actually moving into and out of the business over time.

A simple example

Imagine a small business makes $2,000 in sales during a month. It has $1,200 in expenses, so its simplified profit is $800. But if customers have only paid $1,400 so far and the business has already paid $1,000 in bills, its cash position is not the same thing as its reported profit.

Why the distinction matters

  • Revenue: helps show sales activity and demand.
  • Profit: helps show whether the business is economically sustainable.
  • Cash flow: helps show whether it can pay bills when they come due.

Build a basic weekly habit

Track sales collected, unpaid invoices, bills due, recurring costs and the cash available. Keep business and personal transactions separate where possible, and set aside money for taxes according to your local rules. A qualified tax professional can help with your specific situation.

The signal: A business can show a profit on paper and still face a cash shortage. Track all three measures.

Editorial note: These are educational starting points, not individualized legal, tax, investment or financial advice. Verify details that depend on your location and circumstances.
